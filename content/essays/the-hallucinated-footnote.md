Every historian has had the experience: a footnote that looks right, cites a plausible archive, a plausible box and folder, a plausible date, and leads nowhere. Before 2023, the culprit was usually a tired graduate student or a careless secondary source. Now it is more often a language model, and the difference matters.{{< sidenote >}}This is dummy text for the *AI @ RRCHNM* prototype. Authors, citations, and anecdotes are invented.{{< /sidenote >}}

A model does not *forget* where it found something. It never found anything. It produces text that has the statistical shape of a citation, and the footnote, the historian's most ordinary tool for accountability, turns out to be very easy to imitate.

## The footnote as a promise

Anthony Grafton called the footnote a kind of curious history, a record of the scholar's labor that lets readers retrace it. A footnote is a promise: *you could go and look*. Its authority does not come from its form but from that promise being kept, over and over, across a whole discipline.

{{< pullquote >}}The fabricated footnote does not break the promise. It reveals how rarely anyone checks.{{< /pullquote >}}

What generative AI exposes is less a new kind of dishonesty than an old habit of trust. We read footnotes as signals of care. We rarely follow them.

## What to do about it

The practical answer is not complicated, but it takes effort:

1. Treat every machine-produced citation as a lead, not a source.
2. Verify against a finding aid, catalog, or digitized original *before* it enters your notes.
3. Record in your notes which references came from a model, so you can audit them later.

The harder answer is cultural. If the footnote is going to keep its meaning, historians will need to check each other's work more often than we have been. That is not a crisis. It is the discipline doing its job.
